Four filters, applied in order:

- **Pure sine wave output.** Non-negotiable for anything with a modern PSU. The next section explains why.

## Why pure sine wave is non-negotiable

Every modern PSU worth owning — anything 80 Plus certified — uses active power factor correction. Active PFC circuitry expects a smooth AC waveform. A cheap "simulated sine" UPS outputs a choppy stepped approximation, and when the power cuts and the UPS switches to battery, two things can happen: the PSU's PFC protection trips and the machine reboots mid-outage, or the PSU refuses to run on the stepped waveform at all. Either way, your server goes down hard during the exact event you bought the UPS to protect it from. This isn't theory — it's a well-documented pattern in PSU and UPS forums, and the reason every homelab guide repeats the same two words.

The price gap has mostly closed. The pure sine CP850PFCLCD is $169.95; simulated-sine units in the same class save you maybe $40. That's a bad trade against an unclean shutdown of a ZFS pool or a Proxmox host with running VMs.

## Size by watts, buy runtime

VA numbers are marketing adjacent. The watt rating is what the UPS can actually carry, and it's always lower: the CP1500PFCLCD is 1500VA but 1000W; the BR1500MS2 is 1500VA but 900W. Add up your real draw with a cheap power meter before buying:

- A [mini PC server](/best-mini-pc-home-server/) (N150 class): 8–15W
- Mini PC plus a 4-bay NAS and a 2.5GbE switch: 60–120W
- A Proxmox host with a GPU or a shelf of spinning disks: 150–300W

Here's the part people miss: at those loads, a 1500VA unit isn't working hard at all. You're not buying capacity, you're buying minutes. A CP1500PFCLCD carrying 100W will run the better part of an hour, which is enough to ride out a short blip without shutting anything down and still have margin for a clean shutdown when the outage sticks.

## Wire up graceful shutdown (the part everyone skips)

A UPS without shutdown automation protects against nothing but short blips. The standard tool on Linux is [Network UPS Tools](https://networkupstools.org/). Every CyberPower and APC pick above speaks `usbhid-ups` — same driver, both brands.

On the host with the UPS plugged in (Proxmox, Debian, whatever runs your [Docker stack](/docker-containers-home-server/)):

```bash
apt install nut
```

`/etc/nut/ups.conf`:

```ini
[homeups]
    driver = usbhid-ups
    port = auto
    # vendorid = 0764   # CyberPower; APC is 051d — usually auto-detected
    desc = "Home server UPS"
```

`/etc/nut/upsd.users`:

```ini
[monuser]
    password = pick-something-real
    upsmon master
```

`/etc/nut/upsmon.conf` (the two lines that matter):

```ini
MONITOR homeups@localhost 1 monuser pick-something-real master
SHUTDOWNCMD "/sbin/shutdown -h now"
```

Set `MODE=standalone` in `/etc/nut/nut.conf`, then `systemctl enable --now nut-server nut-monitor` and check that `upsc homeups` shows battery percentage and estimated runtime. Other machines on the same UPS install `nut-client` and monitor `homeups@<server-ip>` as `slave` — one USB cable, every box shuts down in order. TrueNAS, Synology DSM, and UGREEN UGOS all have built-in NUT clients: point them at the same server IP with the same credentials.

By default `upsmon` waits for the UPS to flag a low battery, which on some units happens annoyingly late. Pull the trigger earlier by telling the UPS when to declare it: `upsrw -s battery.runtime.low=300 homeups` flags low battery once estimated runtime drops under five minutes, and `SHUTDOWNCMD` takes it from there. Five minutes of margin is plenty — a clean Proxmox shutdown takes about two.

## Also worth knowing

**Line-interactive vs online.** Everything here is line-interactive: the inverter kicks in when power fails, in 2-6ms. An online (double-conversion) UPS rebuilds the power continuously with zero transfer time and cleaner output, at 2-3x the price and 5-10% more heat. For a home server, line-interactive plus AVR covers the realistic failure modes. Eaton's 9 series is the upgrade when you genuinely need it.

**Budget for the second battery.** Lead-acid fades in 3-5 years whether you use it or not. The CP1500PFCLCD takes a ~$45 pair of 12V/9Ah bricks; APC's genuine RBC packs cost more. Third-party RBC-compatible packs exist and mostly review fine. When runtime at your load halves from the day-one number, it's time.

**Never plug in:** laser printers (current spikes that trip the inverter), space heaters, and anything with a motor. Battery-backed outlets are for the server, the NAS, the switch, and the router. Monitor on surge-only — you don't need it to survive an outage, and it saves minutes.

**One wall outlet, one UPS.** Daisychaining UPSes or power strips into battery outlets confuses the AVR logic and violates every warranty. Plug the UPS directly into the wall.

## FAQ

<Accordion label="What size UPS do I need for a home server?" group="faq">
Size by watts, not VA. Measure your stack with a power meter: a mini PC plus NAS plus switch typically idles at 60-120W. Any 1500VA/1000W unit carries that forever; you're really buying runtime, so a bigger battery bank means more minutes, not more capacity.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Is pure sine wave really necessary?" group="faq">
For anything with an active PFC power supply (every modern 80 Plus PSU), yes. Simulated sine units can trip the PSU's protection the moment they switch to battery, rebooting your server during the outage. Pure sine costs a little more and eliminates the whole failure mode.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="How long will a UPS run my server?" group="faq">
Depends on load, roughly inverse. A 1500VA unit at 100W runs 45-50 minutes; at 300W, 13-16 minutes; at full load, 2-4 minutes. The runtime table above has the numbers per pick. For graceful shutdown purposes, anything over 10 minutes at your real load is enough.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Can a power station like the EcoFlow DELTA 3 replace a UPS?" group="faq">
Partly. It passes power through and switches to battery in 10-20ms, which most network gear and many server PSUs tolerate — but it doesn't speak NUT/USB-HID, so it can't tell your server to shut down when the battery runs low. Use it with Home Assistant scripting shutdowns, or on equipment that reboots cleanly. For pure shutdown protection, a $240 UPS is still the better tool.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="How often do UPS batteries need replacing?" group="faq">
Sealed lead-acid lasts 3-5 years, whether used or not. When runtime at your normal load drops to about half of the day-one figure, replace the batteries. For the CyberPower picks that's a ~$45 pair of 12V/9Ah bricks behind a screw door; APC units take pricier branded or compatible RBC packs.
</Accordion>
