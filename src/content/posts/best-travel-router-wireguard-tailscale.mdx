## Why carry a travel router instead of using a phone hotspot?

A travel router solves three problems that a phone hotspot or per-device VPN app doesn't:

1. **Authenticate the hotel captive portal once.** You connect the travel router to the hotel's Wi-Fi (WISP repeater mode) and complete the room-number + last-name login screen a single time. Your laptop, phone, iPad, Kindle, and Apple TV / Chromecast stay connected to your router's private SSID (`ssid-yours`) all week — no re-authenticating five devices every morning, and no hotel Device Isolation blocking your laptop from talking to your phone or travel streaming stick.
2. **One always-on VPN tunnel for every device (including locked-down work laptops).** If your employer locks down your corporate laptop so you can't install personal WireGuard or Tailscale clients, the travel router runs the tunnel in hardware. Every packet leaving its LAN or Wi-Fi is encrypted before it touches the hotel AP. I tunnel mine back to my home [OPNsense firewall](/best-mini-pc-opnsense/) and mini PC lab so I can reach Jellyfin, Proxmox, and internal SSH hosts on their normal LAN IPs.
3. **Multi-wan fallback: hotel Ethernet, hotel Wi-Fi, or USB phone tethering.** When a conference room still has a live Ethernet jack, you plug it into WAN. When the hotel Wi-Fi dies during a call, you plug your phone into the router's USB 3.0 port for instant USB tethering without changing Wi-Fi networks on any of your devices.

## WireGuard vs Tailscale on a travel router: which should you use?

GL.iNet's newer models (Beryl AX, Slate 7, Beryl 7, Slate AX) ship with both a native **WireGuard Client** and a **Tailscale** toggle under `Applications > Tailscale`. They solve slightly different problems on the road:

| Feature | Native WireGuard Client | Built-in Tailscale Plugin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Speed on Beryl AX** | **~300 Mbps** (kernel-space `kmod-wireguard`) | **~80–130 Mbps** (userspace `tailscaled` overhead) |
| **Requires public IP / port forward at home?** | Yes (UDP port forwarded to home firewall/WG server) | **No** — punches through CGNAT and double-NAT automatically |
| **Hardware kill switch ("Block Non-VPN Traffic")** | **Yes**, first-class and rock-solid | Partial; verify exit-node routing before trusting |
| **Commercial VPN providers (Mullvad, Proton, IVPN)** | Yes (upload any `.conf` file) | Only Mullvad via Tailscale add-on |
| **Best when** | You have a public IP at home or use a commercial VPN | Your home ISP uses CGNAT, or you run [Headscale](/headscale-self-hosted-tailscale-setup/) / Tailscale |

## Hotel Wi-Fi survival guide: fixing the 4 captive-portal traps

Even with a great travel router, hotel and airport networks throw curveballs. Keep these four fixes in your back pocket:

### 1. The hotel login page won't load (DNS Rebinding Protection)

Hotel captive portals work by hijacking a plain HTTP request and redirecting you to a local IP (like `10.1.0.1` or `172.16.0.1`). If you enabled **DNS Rebinding Attack Protection**, **AdGuard Home**, or **Encrypted DNS (DoH/DoT)** on your GL.iNet router, the router blocks that private-IP DNS response as an attack, and the hotel login screen never appears.

**The 30-second hotel check-in routine:**
1. Flip the VPN kill switch **OFF** (using the side toggle button or web UI).
2. In the GL.iNet admin page (`192.168.8.1`), go to **Network > DNS** and temporarily disable **DNS Rebinding Attack Protection** (and set DNS mode to Automatic so the router uses the hotel's DHCP DNS).
3. Open `http://neverssl.com` or `http://captive.apple.com` on your laptop or phone, log into the hotel portal, and flip your WireGuard VPN + DNS protection back **ON**.

### 2. Bypassing stubborn portals with MAC Cloning

On cruise ships, flights, or hotels where captive portals refuse to load through a router, authenticate on your phone's Wi-Fi first. Then connect your phone to the travel router, go to **Network > MAC Address** in the GL.iNet UI, and click **Clone MAC** to copy your phone's MAC address onto the router's WAN/Repeater interface. Once your phone disconnects from the hotel SSID, the hotel equipment thinks your travel router *is* your already-authenticated phone.

### 3. Avoid the `192.168.8.1` and `192.168.1.1` subnet collision

By default, GL.iNet routers use `192.168.8.1/24` for their LAN, and most home networks use `192.168.1.0/24` or `192.168.0.0/24`. If a hotel or Airbnb uses `192.168.1.0/24` on its guest Wi-Fi **and** your home LAN is also `192.168.1.0/24`, you will not be able to reach your home servers over WireGuard because your travel router sends `192.168.1.x` traffic to the local hotel gateway instead of the VPN tunnel.

**Fix before you leave home:** under **Network > LAN** on your travel router, change the router's LAN IP to an uncommon subnet like `10.77.8.1` (`255.255.255.0`), and keep your home lab on an uncommon RFC1918 range (like `10.20.30.0/24`) rather than `192.168.1.0/24`.

### 4. Drop WireGuard MTU to `1380` if SSH or HTTPS hangs

When WireGuard runs inside a hotel's VLAN-tagged or PPPoE network (or over USB cellular tethering), the standard `1420` byte WireGuard MTU can fragment and drop large packets. Symptoms: `ping 1.1.1.1` works, but Slack, SSH `git push`, or web pages hang indefinitely. In your GL.iNet WireGuard client config (`VPN > WireGuard Client`), edit the peer configuration and lower `MTU = 1380` (or `1280` on cellular).

### Power tip: skip the hotel TV USB port

The Beryl AX needs **5V/3A (15W)** over USB-C, and the Wi-Fi 7 Slate 7 wants a proper **5V/3A or 9V/3A PD** feed. Plugging a modern Wi-Fi 6/7 travel router into a 500mA USB-A port on the back of a hotel television causes random reboots the moment the Wi-Fi radio and WireGuard CPU spin up. Power it from the included wall brick, a free USB-C port on your laptop, or a high-output battery like the [Anker 25,000mAh 165W laptop power bank](/go/anker-laptop-power-bank/).

## FAQ

<Accordion label="What does a travel router actually do in a hotel?" group="faq" expanded="true">
A travel router connects to the hotel's Wi-Fi or Ethernet once and broadcasts your own private, encrypted Wi-Fi network for all your devices. You only complete the hotel's captive portal login screen once, your devices can talk to each other without hotel client isolation, and the router can encrypt all traffic through a single WireGuard or Tailscale VPN tunnel back home.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Can a travel router hide that I am working remotely?" group="faq">
When you connect a corporate laptop to a travel router running a WireGuard client connected to your home router (with the kill switch enabled), all internet traffic exits from your residential home IP address. Keep in mind that a corporate laptop with Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, or GPS enabled can still detect nearby BSSIDs for location services unless Wi-Fi/Bluetooth are turned off and you connect the laptop to the travel router's LAN port via an Ethernet cable.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="WireGuard or Tailscale on GL.iNet: which is better?" group="faq">
Native **WireGuard** is two to three times faster (~300 Mbps on the Beryl AX) and integrates directly with GL.iNet's hardware kill switch, making it the first choice if you can forward a UDP port on your home router or use a commercial VPN. **Tailscale** is easier to set up when your home ISP uses CGNAT (no public IPv4 address), though its userspace daemon runs slower on router CPUs.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Is Wi-Fi 7 worth it on a travel router when hotel Wi-Fi is slow?" group="faq">
Not for the hotel WAN link itself, which is usually capped under 50 Mbps. You buy a Wi-Fi 7 model like the Slate 7 or Beryl 7 for the **local** upgrades: dual 2.5GbE ports for fast wired transfers between your laptop and a portable NAS/server, faster CPU cores for ~500+ Mbps WireGuard when plugged into fast venue Ethernet, and cleaner 5GHz MLO links in RF-crowded convention centers.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Can I power a GL.iNet travel router from a power bank or laptop?" group="faq">
Yes. The Beryl AX, Slate 7, Beryl 7, and Opal all run on standard USB-C power (15W 5V/3A on the Beryl AX). A laptop Thunderbolt/USB-C port or any USB-C PD power bank can run the router for hours on a train, flight, or conference table.
</Accordion>
