This comparison first went up in October 2025, when the ASUS DC510 was listing around €420 in Europe and the Razer Thunderbolt 5 Dock Chroma was one of the few Thunderbolt 5 docks you could actually order. In September 2026 both docks are still worth owning, but the buying decision has changed: the ASUS street price is down to roughly $350, the Razer sits around $390, and a $195 Kensington now covers most of what these two do if you can live without an M.2 slot.

Short version first: I would buy the **ASUS DC510** for a desk that moves files over a network or handles camera cards, and I would only buy the **Razer Chroma** if Chroma RGB sync with your other Razer gear is a real requirement, or you want Thunderbolt Share between two machines. Everything else is close enough that the software and the networking decide it, not the marketing.

## Step zero: check your host before you buy either dock

This is the step that decides whether you are buying a $350 dock or a $350 paperweight. Thunderbolt 5 hosts are what unlock the 80 Gbps link, PCIe 4.0 x4 tunneling (which is what makes a dock-mounted NVMe fast) and Bandwidth Boost up to 120 Gbps toward displays. On a Thunderbolt 4 or USB4 host, both docks still work, but you get 40 Gbps, PCIe 3.0 x4, and your host's own display limit.

Check it in 30 seconds.

<Tabs>
<Tab name="macOS">

```bash
# Look for the link speed on the Thunderbolt bus
system_profiler SPThunderboltDataType | grep -E "Thunderbolt|Speed|Link Status"

# How many displays this host can drive, and at what refresh
system_profiler SPDisplaysDataType | grep -E "Resolution|Refresh Rate"
```

**Expected on a Thunderbolt 5 host:** the tree shows the dock with `Speed: Up to 80 Gb/s`. On an M4 Pro or M5 Pro Mac this is what you will see.

**What broken looks like:** `Speed: Up to 40 Gb/s`. That is a Thunderbolt 4 host (M1, M2, M3 Macs, most Intel Macs). Everything works, the dock's M.2 slot just runs at roughly half speed and the third display will not be there.

</Tab>
<Tab name="Windows">

```powershell
Get-PnpDevice -Class System | Where-Object FriendlyName -like "*Thunderbolt*" | Select-Object FriendlyName, Status
Get-PnpDevice -Class USB | Where-Object FriendlyName -like "*USB4*" | Select-Object FriendlyName, Status
```

**Expected:** the Thunderbolt controller appears with status `OK`, and after plugging the dock in, the Thunderbolt Control Center app asks you to approve the new device once.

**What broken looks like:** no Thunderbolt controller at all (you have a plain USB-C port, and both docks drop to USB 3.2 behaviour), or the device sits in `Error` state until you install the current Intel Thunderbolt driver.

</Tab>
<Tab name="Linux">

```bash
boltctl list
lspci | grep -i thunderbolt
lsblk -o NAME,SIZE,MODEL,TRAN
```

**Expected:** `boltctl list` shows the dock with an authorized status, and the NVMe inside the dock shows up as a normal block device.

**Note:** USB4 v2 host support on Linux is still the least predictable of the three platforms. If the kernel does not bring the link up as expected, the dock falls back to working as a USB4/USB hub at reduced bandwidth. Do not buy either dock expecting to debug the link on Linux if you need it working tomorrow.

</Tab>
</Tabs>

If you are on a Thunderbolt 4 host and an M.2 slot is not a hard requirement, stop here and buy the cheap end of the category. You will never touch what these two charge for. Both the [best Thunderbolt 5 docks guide](https://www.bitdoze.com/best-thunderbolt-5-docks-guide/) and my [ASUS DC510 review](https://www.bitdoze.com/asus-thunderbolt-5-dock-dc510-review/) go into the host math in more detail.

The port layouts differ more than anything else here. The ASUS puts the card readers and one USB-A on the front where you can reach them, and it gives you four USB-A ports total. The Razer puts an extra USB-C port on the dock, which matters if your peripherals are USB-C native, but only two USB-A.

## Networking: 2.5GbE vs 1GbE is the whole argument

This is the single difference that changes what you can do at the desk.

| | ASUS DC510 | Razer Chroma |
|---|---|---|
| **Link speed** | 2.5 Gigabit (2500base-T) | 1 Gigabit (1000baseT) |
| **Realistic throughput** | ~280 MB/s | ~110 MB/s |
| **100GB over the wire** | ~6 minutes | ~15 minutes |

The Razer's 1GbE is not a design flaw, it is a market decision. Razer built this dock for gamers, and game downloads do not care much past a gigabit. But if you pull footage off a NAS, run backups to a server, or touch a big repo over the network, 1GbE is a hard wall and the dock cannot fix it with firmware.

Two footguns with the ASUS port:

- **2.5GbE only helps if the path supports it.** A CAT5e run, an old 1G switch, or a cheap USB-C-to-Ethernet adapter in the middle will negotiate down and the dock gets blamed.
- **Verify, do not assume.** After you plug in, check the negotiated speed instead of trusting the listing:

```bash
# macOS
networksetup -listallhardwareports | grep -A 3 -i ethernet
ifconfig en6 | grep media          # en6 = the interface the previous command printed

# Linux
ip -br link                        # find the dock's interface, usually enp...
ethtool enp87s0 | grep -i speed    # swap in the name from the line above

# Windows
Get-NetAdapter | Select-Object Name, LinkSpeed
```

**Expected:** `2500base-T` on macOS, `Speed: 2500Mb/s` from ethtool, `2.5 Gbps` from Get-NetAdapter.

**What broken looks like:** `1000baseT` or `1 Gbps`. That is your cable, switch or patch panel, not the dock.

<Notice type="success" title="The one case where 1GbE is fine">
If your internet is 1Gb or slower and your only network traffic is that internet connection, the Razer's 1GbE port costs you nothing. The difference only shows up between machines on your own LAN: NAS transfers, local backups, VM images, container pulls from a local registry.
</Notice>

If you do go ASUS and want the full 2.5G path, budget for a 2.5GbE switch. Unmanaged 5-port models run roughly $40 to 80 as of this writing, which is cheap next to what a 10GbE setup costs.

<Notice type="warning" title="The Thunderbolt 4 ceiling">
Put the same SSD in either dock on a Thunderbolt 4 host and it stalls around 2.6 to 3 GB/s, because TB4 only tunnels PCIe 3.0 x4. If you see those numbers on an M1, M2 or M3 Mac, the dock is not the problem.

If you are buying a drive for the slot, the [WD_BLACK SN850X 2TB](/go/wd-black-sn850x-2tb/) is what I used and it has held up. Two things before you install a drive that already has data on it: macOS mounts NTFS read-only, and reformatting erases the drive. Copy anything you care about off first, then pick the filesystem on purpose (APFS for Mac only, exFAT if you move between Mac and Windows).

## Displays

On paper, identical: triple 4K at 144Hz or dual 8K at 60Hz on both, and both need Display Stream Compression to get there.

| Configuration | ASUS DC510 | Razer Chroma |
|--------------|-----------|--------------|
| Triple 4K @144Hz | Yes, with DSC | Yes, with DSC |
| Dual 8K @60Hz | Yes, with DSC | Yes, with DSC |
| My tested config | Dual 4K @144Hz (M1 Pro), dual 4K @165Hz (M4 Pro) | Not tested |

Worth understanding the math, because it explains most "the dock is broken" complaints. One 4K display at 144Hz and 10-bit color is roughly 36 Gbps of pixel data before overhead. Three of those is around 107 Gbps, which does not fit in an 80 Gbps link, so the spec sheet depends on DSC on the host GPU plus Bandwidth Boost. Dual 8K at 60Hz leans on DSC too.

So if a monitor drops to 60Hz or a third display refuses to wake, check the host's maximum display count and the cable before you blame the dock. My M1 Pro drives two external displays, full stop.

If you are pairing a dock with a high refresh panel, my [ASUS ROG Strix OLED XG32UCWG review](https://www.bitdoze.com/asus-rog-strix-oled-xg32ucwg-review/) covers what 4K at 165Hz feels like in daily work, and the [best 32-inch OLED monitors guide](https://www.bitdoze.com/best-32-inch-oled-monitors-guide/) has the current picks.

## Ops notes: a dock is infrastructure

Two docks, same operational reality. A few things I would think about before making either one part of a daily workflow.

**It is a single point of failure for your desk.** One cable and one power supply sit between you and both monitors, your network and your storage. Keep the Thunderbolt cable that came in the box as a spare, or buy a second certified one. A marginal cable presents as random display dropouts and slower-than-expected storage rather than an obvious failure, which is the worst kind of fault to debug.

**The SSD in the dock is not a backup.** It is a removable, dock-resident drive that a power fault, a bad unplug or a dropped bag can take out. Whatever lives on it needs a second copy somewhere else. For the offsite leg I keep pointing people at cheap S3-compatible object storage; [Bunny Storage](https://go.bitdoze.com/bunny) is one of the cheaper options, and [Bunny vs S3 vs Backblaze](https://www.bitdoze.com/bunny-storage-vs-s3-vs-backblaze/) compares the pricing. If you would rather keep it on your own hardware, [Pluton with Restic and Rclone](https://www.bitdoze.com/pluton-self-hosted-backup/) covers the same job.

**Firmware and warranty.** Before deciding a dock is defective, check the vendor support page for a firmware release. On macOS the docks are driverless, so a firmware update has to happen from a Windows host. And read the warranty term for your region before importing: a cross-border RMA on a $350 accessory takes weeks.

**Monitor the things that depend on it.** If the dock's NIC or SSD matters to your work, a quick uptime check on whatever service sits at the other end of that link will tell you when the path breaks. The pattern I use is [Beszel and Uptime Kuma on Dokploy](https://www.bitdoze.com/beszel-uptime-kuma/).

## Frequently asked questions

<Accordion label="Is the Razer dock quieter than the ASUS DC510?" group="faq">
On the balance of reports, yes. I have measured the ASUS fan myself (audible across a quiet room, spins up with a single monitor attached) and I have not measured the Razer, so I am not going to put a decibel figure on either. If silence is a hard requirement, look at passively cooled docks like the Kensington SD5000T5 instead of either of these.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Can I use either dock with an M1, M2 or M3 Mac?" group="faq">
Yes, both work, but you get Thunderbolt 4 behaviour: 40 Gbps, PCIe 3.0 x4 tunneling (so the internal SSD caps around 2.6 to 3 GB/s), and your Mac's display limit on top. The ports, Ethernet, card readers, charging and the M.2 slot all still function. You are just paying for bandwidth you cannot use.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Do I need to buy a Thunderbolt 5 cable?" group="faq">
No, both docks ship with one, and the ASUS includes a 100cm TB5 cable. Keep it. If you want a spare, buy a certified Thunderbolt 5 cable rather than a generic USB-C cable claiming 80 Gbps. An uncertified cable presents as intermittent display dropouts or storage that is slower than the reviews promised, and it is the first thing to swap when something is flaky.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Which dock is better for dual 8K?" group="faq">
On paper the ASUS, since it explicitly lists dual 8K at 60Hz alongside triple 4K at 144Hz. In practice both need DSC on the host GPU and an 80 Gbps host link, so if you are on a Thunderbolt 4 Mac neither one will do it. Check the host first, then the cable, then the dock.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Can I boot from the SSD in the dock?" group="faq">
Not something I would do on a machine I rely on. It works on some configurations, but you have just made your boot drive depend on a removable device, a single cable and a dock's power supply. Keep the internal drive as the boot volume and treat the dock SSD as scratch space.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Does the Razer dock work with non-Razer laptops?" group="faq">
Yes. Chroma lighting needs Razer Synapse to configure, but the dock itself works with any Thunderbolt 4, Thunderbolt 5 or USB4 host, including Macs. You lose the RGB control, not the ports.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Do I actually need 2.5GbE?" group="faq">
Only if you move large files between machines on your own network. For internet-only traffic at 1Gb or below, the Razer's 1GbE port costs you nothing. For NAS transfers, local backups and pulling VM or container images from a local registry, 2.5GbE is roughly 2.5x faster and the ASUS is the only one of these two that offers it.
</Accordion>

## Final verdict

**Default answer: ASUS DC510.** It costs less, has more ports, gives you 2.5GbE and dual card readers, and its M.2 bay is the easier one to live with. On a Thunderbolt 5 host it is a genuinely fast dock. The fan noise is the price of admission, and it is the only thing I would warn a friend about before ordering.

**Razer Thunderbolt 5 Dock Chroma is the right buy for one specific desk:** Razer peripherals everywhere, a Chroma profile you care about, and a network that tops out at 1Gb. Add Thunderbolt Share if you switch one desk between a work laptop and a personal machine. Outside that scenario you are paying more for fewer ports.

**And if you do not need the M.2 slot:** buy the Kensington SD5000T5 at $195 to 300, pocket the difference, and enjoy the passive cooling.
