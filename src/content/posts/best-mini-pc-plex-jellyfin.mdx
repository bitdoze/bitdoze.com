## Transcodes per watt: why Intel Quick Sync wins this

Two facts decide almost everything in this category.

First, Plex charges for the feature this article is about. Hardware-accelerated streaming requires an active Plex Pass ([Plex support](https://support.plex.tv/articles/115002178853-using-hardware-accelerated-streaming/)). Jellyfin's hardware transcoding is free. If that difference alone decides for you, skip ahead: any box here runs Jellyfin, and Intel versus AMD stops mattering much because Jellyfin's VAAPI path works on both.

Second, the reliable cheap path is Intel Quick Sync. On the Plex side the same support article says Plex has "technical support for many dedicated AMD graphics cards, but we haven't done official, full testing," and in practice it shows: in a Plex forum thread from December 2025 an 8845HS user reports hardware transcoding works for SDR but HDR tone mapping falls back to software ([Plex forums](https://forums.plex.tv/t/plex-sever-docker-not-using-gpu-to-transcode-both-intel-b580-and-amd-mi50/934841)). Software tone mapping on 4K HDR is the single heaviest job a media server does, so "works" without it is a trap. On Jellyfin, AMD over VAAPI is a supported and common path, so the same AMD box is fine there.

That is why every pick in this list is Intel. For AMD-based homelab boxes that do other jobs, see the [best mini PC for a home server](/best-mini-pc-home-server/) roundup.

## My Plex box, and where it gives up

My own Plex server runs on an Intel N95 mini PC with 16GB of RAM that I bought a few years ago for about $100. For 1080p and direct play it has been fine for years. 4K HDR is where it gives up: a tone-mapped 4K stream is the one job it can't do well, and it's the workload that separates the tiers below.

Worth knowing before you assume an N150 fixes that: the N150's UHD graphics is a mild step up, not a new tier. Intel's ARK lists the N95 at 16 execution units with a 1.2GHz max graphics clock, while the N150 bumps the count to 24 EUs but drops the clock to 1.0GHz ([Intel ARK N95](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/products/sku/231800/intel-processor-n95-6m-cache-up-to-3-40-ghz/specifications.html), [Intel ARK N150](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/products/sku/241636/intel-processor-n150-6m-cache-up-to-3-60-ghz/specifications.html)). The net edge mildly favors the N150, so it transcodes a little faster, but 4K HDR tone mapping is still the job it does least well, and that's why the Core Ultra picks exist.

## How many streams each tier handles

Approximate simultaneous 4K HEVC 10-bit to 1080p transcodes, my estimates extrapolated from published N100/N95 Quick Sync benchmarks since the N150 is the same silicon class. Treat each cell as "about this many," not a promise:

| Tier | Without tone mapping | With HDR tone mapping |
| --- | --- | --- |
| N150 (UHD Graphics) | ~2-3 | ~1-2 |
| Core Ultra 5 125U (Arc) | ~3-4 | ~2-3 |
| Core Ultra 200H (Arc) | ~4-5 | ~3 |
| i9-13900H (Iris Xe) | ~4-6 | ~3-4 |

If your household reality is one 4K stream and a couple of 1080p clients, every box here clears it. The N150 only breaks when several people transcode HDR at once, and direct play doesn't hit the GPU at all, so buy for the worst evening, not the average one.

On AV1: the N150's UHD graphics decode AV1 but can't encode it. Core Ultra chips from Meteor Lake onward (the 125U in the K15, the 200H in the NUC 15 Pro) encode AV1. For Plex and Jellyfin that mostly matters for recordings and future-proofing: a server transcode usually outputs H.264 for client compatibility, and AV1 encode on the box only pays off once your whole client chain can decode it.

Where the tone-mapping toggles live, since half the guides bury them: in Jellyfin it's Dashboard > Playback > Transcoding, where you pick the tone-mapping algorithm (VPP or OpenCL depending on your hardware path) and enable HDR to SDR conversion. In Plex it's Settings > Transcoder and the "Tone mapping" checkbox, which costs serious CPU or GPU headroom and is the single reason the N150 ceiling drops to a couple of streams.

## Where the library lives

None of these boxes should hold the actual library. Three sane patterns:

- Internal expansion first. The EQ14's second M.2 slot and the S13's 2.5-inch bay take a spare drive, the K15 and NUCs take bigger NVMe, and the MS-01 has three M.2 slots. Fine for a few TB, wrong answer for a media collection that grows.
- A USB DAS next to the box, when you want cheap local disks without a second machine.
- A NAS share over NFS or SMB, which is the right long-term answer. The [NAS and home-server hardware](/best-nas-homelab-docker-proxmox/) guide covers the options, and a [Samba share setup](/setup-samba-linux/) takes an evening.

On networking: gigabit carries a 4K remux fine (peak bitrates sit around 100-120Mbps), so 2.5GbE only matters once the box doubles as a NAS or multiple remuxes run at once. If you do go multi-gig, the [2.5GbE and 10GbE switch](/best-2-5-10gbe-switch-homelab/) roundup has the short list.

## Turn on hardware transcoding

In Plex, the toggle lives under Settings > Transcoder as "Use hardware acceleration when available," and it requires Plex Pass. In Docker, map the render device through:

```yaml
services:
  plex:
    devices:
      - /dev/dri:/dev/dri
```

For Jellyfin the full working compose with the render group GID is in the [self-hosted Jellyfin guide](/self-host-jellyfin/). The short version:

```yaml
services:
  jellyfin:
    devices:
      - /dev/dri/renderD128:/dev/dri/renderD128
```

Then pick Intel QuickSync (QSV) in Dashboard > Playback > Transcoding. One last thing: a media box transcoding for the household deserves a small UPS so a blip doesn't kill a library mid-write; the [best UPS for a home server](/best-ups-for-home-server/) guide covers the ~$170 answer.

## FAQ

<Accordion label="Is an N100 or N150 mini PC good enough for Plex?" group="faq" expanded="true">
Yes for most homes. An N150 transcodes about 2-3 simultaneous 4K HEVC streams to 1080p through Quick Sync, by my estimate from N100-class benchmarks, and direct play costs almost nothing. You only outgrow it with several concurrent HDR streams.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Do I need Plex Pass for hardware transcoding?" group="faq">
Yes. Plex gates hardware-accelerated streaming behind Plex Pass, per its support article. Jellyfin's hardware transcoding is free, which is the single biggest reason to pick Jellyfin on cheap hardware.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="How many 4K transcodes can an N150 handle?" group="faq">
Roughly two to three 4K HEVC 10-bit to 1080p streams without tone mapping, and about one to two with HDR tone mapping, by my estimate extrapolated from N100-class Quick Sync benchmarks. Treat that as an estimate; bitrate and tone-mapping settings move the number.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Intel or AMD for Jellyfin?" group="faq">
Both work on Linux, AMD through VAAPI and Intel through QSV. Intel is still the safer pick because Plex's AMD support is unofficial and a [Plex forum thread](https://forums.plex.tv/t/plex-sever-docker-not-using-gpu-to-transcode-both-intel-b580-and-amd-mi50/934841) documents HDR tone mapping falling back to software on AMD iGPUs.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Does Plex support AMD GPUs?" group="faq">
Officially, no. Plex's support article says it has "technical support for many dedicated AMD graphics cards" without full testing, and forum reports show SDR hardware transcoding working while tone mapping drops to software. If Plex is the target, buy Intel.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Mini PC or NAS for Plex?" group="faq">
A mini PC transcodes better per dollar than almost any NAS CPU, so the strong pattern is a small PC for Plex plus cheap storage behind it. An MS-01-class box covers both roles if you want one machine.
</Accordion>
