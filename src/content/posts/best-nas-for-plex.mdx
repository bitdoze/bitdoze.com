## The spec that decides everything: Quick Sync (and Plex vs Jellyfin)

A media server does one of two things with a video file. Direct play means it hands the file to the client untouched and the client decodes it, which costs the server almost nothing. Transcoding means the server re-encodes the video so a weaker client, a phone on hotel Wi-Fi, or a TV that can't read HEVC 10-bit gets something it can play. Direct play is free. Transcoding is the job that costs you, and on a NAS it belongs to the integrated GPU, not the processor cores.

Two facts shape your hardware choice here:

1. **Plex charges for hardware transcoding; Jellyfin includes it for free.** Plex gates Intel Quick Sync hardware acceleration behind an active Plex Pass ([Plex support](https://support.plex.tv/articles/115002178853-using-hardware-accelerated-streaming/)). Jellyfin's QSV and VAAPI hardware transcoding, including 4K HDR-to-SDR tone mapping, is completely free under GPL-2.0. On a $340-$365 budget NAS, skipping the Plex Pass paywall is the single biggest reason people run Jellyfin in Docker instead. I went through the full feature trade-off in the [mini PC Plex and Jellyfin guide](/best-mini-pc-plex-jellyfin/) and the [self-hosted Jellyfin guide](/self-host-jellyfin/).
2. **Even on Jellyfin, the AMD and ARM NAS picks here cannot hardware-transcode.** On mini PCs, Jellyfin users can buy an AMD Ryzen APU (like a Ryzen 7 8845HS) and transcode over VAAPI. On a NAS, you don't get an APU:
   - **Synology DS925+** ships an embedded AMD Ryzen V1500B with **no integrated GPU on the silicon at all**. Unlike desktop Ryzen chips with Radeon graphics, the V1500B has zero GPU cores. Both Plex and Jellyfin are forced into software transcoding on four Zen 1 cores, where a single 4K HDR stream pushes the CPU to its knees.
   - **Synology DS223j** runs an ARM Realtek chip with no video engine Plex or Jellyfin can use, plus 1GB of soldered RAM (below Jellyfin 12.x's 2GB floor).

Two other details catch NAS buyers off guard once they start streaming 4K Blu-ray remuxes:

- **Subtitle burn-in (PGS and ASS) still hits a single CPU thread.** If a TV or browser client cannot direct-play image-based PGS subtitles (standard on Blu-ray rips) or complex ASS anime subtitles, Plex and Jellyfin have to burn those subtitles into the video frames on the CPU even while Quick Sync handles the video encode. That single-core bottleneck is where the Pentium Gold 8505 (DXP4800 Plus) and Core i3-N305 (F4-424 Pro) pull far ahead of the 2019 Celeron J4125 in the DS225+.
- **M.2 NVMe slots matter for the Plex and Jellyfin database, not the movies.** Your MKV files belong on spinning hard drives, but Plex's `/config` directory (`Library/Application Support/Plex Media Server`) and Jellyfin's `/config` + `/cache` directories (SQLite database, poster art, and trickplay/chapter thumbnails) consist of hundreds of thousands of tiny files. Putting those paths on an M.2 NVMe SSD makes poster grids load immediately on an Apple TV or Shield and lets your hard drives spin down when nobody is watching. Every UGREEN and TerraMaster pick here includes dual M.2 NVMe slots; the Synology DS225+ and DS223j have none.

## How many drives does a Plex library need?

More terabytes than people expect, and fewer bays than the spec sheets suggest. A 4K Blu-ray remux runs 50-80GB; a typical 1080p encode is 5-15GB. Twelve terabytes of usable space holds roughly 150-200 4K remuxes or well over a thousand 1080p titles. For most households, starting with two 12-20TB drives leaves plenty of room to grow.

The honest take on RAID for media: a Plex library is re-rippable data, not irreplaceable family photos. RAID 1 on a two-bay box gives you drive-failure uptime but halves capacity; a 4-bay box in RAID 5 (or Synology SHR) only costs one drive to parity instead of half your array. What never works in a NAS is SMR (shingled magnetic recording) drives in any RAID or ZFS pool, because rebuilds stall for days and that is when a second drive dies. Stick strictly to CMR lines: Seagate IronWolf / IronWolf Pro, Seagate Exos, WD Red Plus / Red Pro, or Toshiba N300.

## Turning on hardware transcoding (Plex & Jellyfin Docker Compose)

Whatever Intel NAS you buy, the fastest and cleanest way to run Plex or Jellyfin is in Docker with three storage paths separated: `/config` (and `/cache` for Jellyfin) on your NVMe SSD pool, `/dev/shm` (RAM disk) for temporary transcode chunks so they don't wear out your SSD or thrash your hard drives, and `/data/media` on your spinning HDD array.

On UGREEN (UGOS Docker app), TerraMaster, or Synology Container Manager, pick the `compose.yaml` for your server:

<Tabs>
<Tab name="Plex (Docker Compose)">

```yaml
services:
  plex:
    image: lscr.io/linuxserver/plex:latest
    container_name: plex
    network_mode: host
    environment:
      - PUID=1000
      - PGID=1000
      - TZ=Etc/UTC
      - VERSION=docker
      - PLEX_CLAIM= # optional: paste token from plex.tv/claim on first boot
    volumes:
      - /mnt/nvme/docker/plex-config:/config      # Put on NVMe SSD pool!
      - /dev/shm:/transcode                       # Transcode scratch in RAM
      - /mnt/storage/media:/data/media            # Bulk MKVs on HDD RAID pool
    devices:
      - /dev/dri:/dev/dri                         # Passes Intel Quick Sync iGPU through
    restart: unless-stopped
```

Once the container is up, open the Plex web UI (`http://NAS-IP:32400/web`) and go to **Settings > Transcoder**:
1. Tick **Use hardware acceleration when available** and **Use hardware-accelerated video encoding** (requires an active Plex Pass).
2. Tick **Enable HDR tone mapping** if your clients watch 4K HDR content on SDR screens. Leave it enabled on the DXP4800 Plus, F4-424 Pro, and DXP6800 Pro; on the DS225+ (J4125) test whether your typical stream count holds up.
3. Set **Transcoder temporary directory** to `/transcode` so it uses the `/dev/shm` RAM mount above.

</Tab>
<Tab name="Jellyfin (Docker Compose)">

```yaml
services:
  jellyfin:
    image: jellyfin/jellyfin:12.1
    container_name: jellyfin
    user: 1000:1000
    group_add:
      - "105"                                     # Replace with your NAS 'render' group GID (getent group render)
    network_mode: host
    volumes:
      - /mnt/nvme/docker/jellyfin-config:/config  # Put database & metadata on NVMe SSD!
      - /mnt/nvme/docker/jellyfin-cache:/cache    # Trickplay & image cache on NVMe SSD
      - /dev/shm:/transcode                       # Transcode scratch in RAM
      - /mnt/storage/media:/data/media:ro         # Bulk MKVs on HDD RAID pool
    devices:
      - /dev/dri/renderD128:/dev/dri/renderD128   # Passes Intel Quick Sync render node
    restart: unless-stopped
```

Once the container is up, open the Jellyfin web UI (`http://NAS-IP:8096`) and go to **Dashboard > Playback > Transcoding**:
1. Set **Hardware acceleration** to **Intel QuickSync (QSV)** and set the QSV Device to `/dev/dri/renderD128`.
2. Tick hardware decoding for **H264, HEVC, MPEG2, VC1, VP9,HEVC 10bit, VP9 10bit**, and **AV1** (skip AV1 on the Synology DS225+ Celeron J4125).
3. Tick **Enable Hardware Encoding** and **Enable Tone Mapping** (plus **Enable VPP Tone Mapping** on Intel 11th-gen/N100 and newer).
4. Set **Transcode Path** to `/transcode` so temporary HLS segments stay in `/dev/shm` RAM. Full reverse-proxy and backup steps are in the [self-hosted Jellyfin guide](/self-host-jellyfin/).

</Tab>
</Tabs>

*(On the Synology DS225+ without NVMe slots, map `/config` to `/volume1/docker/plex/config` or `/volume1/docker/jellyfin/config` instead. If you prefer Synology's native Package Center install for Plex over Docker, download the latest `.spk` with "Intel 64-bit" directly from plex.tv/media-server-downloads, as Synology's built-in Package Center repo lags behind.)*

One last thing: a media box transcoding for the household deserves a UPS so a brief power cut doesn't corrupt a RAID array or SQLite database mid-write. The [best UPS for a home server](/best-ups-for-home-server/) guide covers the ~$170 answer.

## FAQ

<Accordion label="Does a NAS need Plex Pass for hardware transcoding, or is Jellyfin free?" group="faq" expanded="true">
Plex requires an active Plex Pass for hardware-accelerated streaming, per its support article. Software transcoding works without it, but a NAS CPU will choke on 4K without Quick Sync. Jellyfin includes Intel Quick Sync (QSV) hardware transcoding and 4K HDR tone mapping for free on the exact same `/dev/dri` device, which makes it a natural pairing for budget NAS hardware like the $340 UGREEN DXP2800.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Should I buy the UGREEN DXP2800 or Synology DS225+ for Plex and Jellyfin?" group="faq">
For Plex or Jellyfin performance per dollar, buy the UGREEN DXP2800 ($339.99): its Intel N100 is faster than the DS225+'s Celeron J4125, supports AV1 decode, ships with 8GB DDR5 instead of 2GB DDR4, and includes two M.2 NVMe slots so your database, poster art, and Jellyfin trickplay cache don't sit on slow hard drives. Buy the Synology DS225+ ($364.29) only if you specifically want Synology's DSM operating system and apps (Synology Photos, Active Backup, SHR) and only need one 4K transcode at a time.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Can the Synology DS925+ run Plex or Jellyfin with hardware transcoding?" group="faq">
It installs and runs both fine, and direct play to local clients (Apple TV 4K, Nvidia Shield) works without a hitch. However, unlike AMD desktop APUs, the embedded Ryzen V1500B in the DS925+ has no integrated GPU on the silicon at all. Neither Plex nor Jellyfin (via VAAPI) can do hardware transcoding on it. Any video transcode runs in software on four CPU cores: enough for a single 1080p stream, not enough for 4K HDR. If you want a 4-bay NAS that transcodes, buy the UGREEN DXP4800 Plus ($657) or pair the DS925+ with an Intel N150 mini PC.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Why do M.2 NVMe slots matter on a Plex or Jellyfin NAS?" group="faq">
Movies and TV episodes stream sequentially and run fine from spinning hard drives. Plex's `/config` directory and Jellyfin's `/config` + `/cache` directories, however, contain a SQLite database and hundreds of thousands of tiny poster images, chapter thumbnails, and trickplay files. Putting those paths on an M.2 NVMe SSD makes library browsing feel instant on your TV and allows the mechanical hard drives to spin down when no media is playing.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Is a 2-bay NAS enough for Plex or Jellyfin?" group="faq">
For most libraries, yes. Two 12-20TB drives in RAID 1 give you 12-20TB usable, which holds roughly 150-300 4K Blu-ray remuxes or thousands of 1080p movies. The main trade-off on a 2-bay box is that RAID 1 costs 50% of your drive capacity for redundancy, whereas a 4-bay NAS in RAID 5 only uses 25% for parity.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Does Plex or Jellyfin on a NAS support AV1?" group="faq">
Hardware AV1 decode works in both Plex and Jellyfin on the newer Intel iGPUs here (the N100 in the DXP2800, Pentium 8505 in the DXP4800 Plus, i3-N305 in the F4-424 Pro, and i5-1235U in the DXP6800 Pro), so those boxes can transcode AV1 source files to H.264/HEVC for older clients. The Celeron J4125 in the Synology DS225+ does not decode AV1 in hardware.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Do I need 10GbE on a Plex or Jellyfin NAS?" group="faq">
No. Even an untouched 4K Blu-ray remux peaks around 100-120Mbps, so gigabit Ethernet has plenty of headroom for streaming. 2.5GbE speeds up moving rips onto the NAS, and the DXP4800 Plus's 10GbE port earns its keep when the NAS also serves a Proxmox cluster or video editing workstation.
</Accordion>
