### Oracle halved the always free Ampere A1 tier

Oracle changed the Always Free documentation between June 3 and June 14, 2026. Wayback snapshots from June 3 still show "3,000 OCPU hours and 18,000 GB hours per month, equivalent to 4 OCPUs and 24 GB of memory." The live page now says "1,500 OCPU hours and 9,000 GB hours, equivalent to 2 OCPUs and 12 GB."

No announcement. Just a quiet docs edit, covered by Linuxiac (June 14), heise (June 16), InfoQ (July 3), and news.lavx.hu (July 3).

What this means in practice:

- A pair of 1 OCPU / 6 GB instances fits the new ceiling. A single 4 OCPU / 24 GB instance does not.
- Free-only accounts risk shutdown if A1 usage exceeds the new limits. Pay-as-you-go (PAYG) accounts risk being billed for the overage instead.
- If you terminate an oversized instance, Oracle's docs warn it "may fail to return above the updated Always Free limit." Deleting and recreating can cost you the capacity permanently.
- PAYG enforcement is ambiguous. Community reports say Oracle support told PAYG customers the old 4/24 allowance still applies, while the public docs say 1,500/9,000 for all tenancies. I cannot resolve this either way. Check your own tenancy's limits in the console.

### Hetzner repriced cloud servers twice

Hetzner announced increases in late February 2026, citing "drastic" component and RAM costs. The first adjustment hit April 1 and applied to new and existing servers (average ~33%). The second landed June 15 with reported increases "up to 200%" on some plans (heise, Cybernews, The Stack).

Prices are now per-datacenter. The same plan can cost different monthly amounts in Falkenstein, Ashburn, or Singapore. Existing servers were repriced both times. If you budgeted around the old numbers, re-check your invoices.

The April and June increases are part of a broader 2026 VPS pricing reset I covered in [VPS price increases in 2026 and when a mini PC wins](/vps-price-increases/).

## What the numbers show

### CPU: Geekbench 6

Nearly identical. Oracle scored 1102/3670 and Hetzner scored 1064/3376 (measured Feb 2026). Same Altra cores, similar clocks, shared tenancy on both sides. CPU is not the differentiator here, and it never was. For the broader picture of ARM versus x86 for WordPress, see [ARM vs x86 VPS benchmarks for WordPress](/arm-vs-x86-vps-server-benchmarks/).

### Disk: NVMe vs network-attached boot volume

This is where the comparison gets decisive. Hetzner's CAX instances use local NVMe storage. Reads hit 2.5 GB/s. Oracle's free tier runs on network-attached block volumes (iSCSI-style) with default 10 VPUs/GB performance. Reads measured around 55 MB/s.

For databases, WooCommerce, or anything that does frequent small reads and writes, this gap matters more than CPU. A busy WordPress site on 55 MB/s disk will feel slow in ways Geekbench does not capture. Hetzner's local NVMe makes the same workload feel instant.

### Network and latency

Hetzner is faster with lower latency to European endpoints. Oracle's free-tier bandwidth scales with OCPU count, and at 2 OCPU it gets less network than the 4 OCPU box I tested in February.

One nuance: Oracle's egress allowance is generous (10 TB/month outbound). The bottleneck is throughput, not a tiny data cap. If you are pushing heavy traffic from Oracle to US users, consider that Hetzner now has US locations (ASH1, HIL1) with different latency profiles.

<Notice type="info" title="Compare, don't expect byte-identical">

<Tabs>
  <Tab name="YABS (all-in-one)">
```bash
curl -sL https://yabs.sh | bash -s -- -g
```
Runs Geekbench, fio disk tests, and iperf3 network tests in one shot. Output format matches the screenshots above.
  </Tab>
  <Tab name="Disk (fio)">
```bash
# Sequential read (the 2.5 GB/s vs 55 MB/s headline)
fio --name=seqread --filename=/tmp/fio.bin --size=1G \
    --rw=read --bs=1M --direct=1 --iodepth=8 \
    --runtime=30 --group_reporting

# Random 4K read (what databases actually do)
fio --name=randread4k --filename=/tmp/fio.bin --size=1G \
    --rw=randread --bs=4k --direct=1 --iodepth=32 \
    --runtime=30 --group_reporting
```
  </Tab>
  <Tab name="CPU (Geekbench 6)">
```bash
# Geekbench 6 for Linux aarch64
# Record the version number you use
./Geekbench-6.3.0-LinuxARMPreview/aarch64/geekbench6 --upload
```
Note the CPU governor and steal time on shared cores for honest comparison.
  </Tab>
  <Tab name="Network (iperf3)">
```bash
# To Hetzner's own speed test endpoint (EU)
iperf3 -c speedtest.fsn.hetzner.com -p 5201 -P 4

# Or the generic Hetzner endpoint
iperf3 -c iperf.hetzner.com
```
  </Tab>
  <Tab name="Oracle limits (oci CLI)">
```bash
# Check YOUR tenancy's actual Always Free ceiling
# (PAYG may show different values than free-only)
oci limits quota list --service-name compute --all
```
This shows the OCPU and memory quotas your account actually has, which may differ from what the docs say depending on when you created the tenancy.

## FAQ

<Accordion label="Is Oracle's free ARM tier still 4 OCPU / 24 GB?" group="faq">
No. Oracle cut the Always Free Ampere A1 allotment to 2 OCPU / 12 GB (1,500 OCPU-hours + 9,000 GB-hours per month) around June 10, 2026. The change was made quietly in the documentation. Wayback snapshots from June 3, 2026 still show the old 4/24 numbers. See Oracle's [Always Free Resources](https://docs.oracle.com/en-us/iaas/Content/FreeTier/freetier_topic-Always_Free_Resources.htm) page for current details. There is an unresolved question about whether PAYG tenancies are held to different limits than free-only accounts; the docs say one thing, community reports of support replies say another.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Will my existing 4 OCPU / 24 GB instance keep running?" group="faq">
Unverified. Community reports are conflicting. Some users report their existing instances continuing to run at the old specs; others report being forced down. If you have a pre-June 2026 instance, check your tenancy's actual limits in the OCI console. Do not terminate and recreate an oversized instance under the new rules, as Oracle's docs warn it may not be possible to provision it again.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="How do I avoid Oracle idle reclamation?" group="faq">
Oracle deems an instance idle if, over a 7-day window, the 95th-percentile CPU, network, and memory utilization are all below 20% (memory rule applies to A1 shapes). To stay above the threshold, keep sustained load on the box: run real workloads, health checks, or scheduled jobs. If your workload is genuinely idle, move it to a provider that does not reclaim. This is an official policy, not a rumor.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Why did Hetzner's prices change twice in 2026?" group="faq">
Hetzner cited "drastic" component and RAM costs (the so-called "RAMpocalypse" from AI demand). The first increase took effect April 1, 2026 (~33% average, applied to existing servers). A second adjustment landed June 15, 2026 with some plans reportedly going up 200-300%. Prices now vary by datacenter location. This is why I treat price-increase risk as a first-class factor when recommending where to run production.
</Accordion>

<Accordion label="Was the benchmark run on a CAX31?" group="faq">
I am not 100% certain. The Geekbench multi-core score (3376 vs 1064 single-core, a ~3.1x ratio) is what a 4-core Altra box scores, not an 8-core one. The benchmarks may have been run on a CAX21 and mislabeled as CAX31. The disk and network results (the headline claims) are unaffected by which CAX plan it was: local NVMe is fast on all of them. If you need certainty, re-run the commands in [How I Tested](#how-i-tested-reproduce-the-benchmarks) on a known plan.
</Accordion>

## Verdict

CPU parity between Oracle ARM and Hetzner ARM is a property of the silicon and that has not changed. What changed is the deal around the hardware. Oracle's free box halved to 2 OCPU / 12 GB with reclamation risk and slow disk. Hetzner's CAX31 is 8 cores / 16 GB NVMe at €20.99–21.49/month (when Cost-Optimized stock is open), with the caveat that Hetzner has shown it will reprice running servers when component costs move.

For production WordPress, databases, or anything disk-bound: Hetzner CAX wins and it is not close. For throwaway work, staging, or learning where you can tolerate losing the instance: Oracle's free tier is still worth using. Just go in with your eyes open about the strings attached.
